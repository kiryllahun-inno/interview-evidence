How to read this page

Every claim carries a label saying how much weight it can bear. A lot of interview advice fails not because it is invented but because a modest finding gets repeated until it sounds like a law.

verified read directly from the source — the table, the page, the printed number  ·  abstract-level confirmed from the publisher's abstract; full text not page-checked  ·  inference a reasonable step beyond what the study tested — ours, not theirs  ·  guardrail a limit on what the finding can say

Verification status. The 18 core sources were read in full; every statistic was checked at page and table level. The 23 newer sources (2010–2026) were verified through publishers' abstracts and bibliographic databases — real numbers, resolving DOIs, but not the same page-level check. 01Can anyone tell when you're lying?

The eye-movement rule · faces as emotion readouts · Lie to Me · what actually works

The rule was tested three ways in 2012 — and failed all three verified

Study 1: 32 people filmed lying and telling the truth; two blind coders scored every eye movement with sound off. Lying: 0.019 up-right gazes/sec. Truth: 0.017.

Study 2: 21 people trained in the rule vs 29 untrained judged 32 clips (16 lies; coin flip = 16 correct). Untrained 16.59, trained 16.33 — slightly worse. Confidence didn't move either: 4.65 vs 4.58 out of 7.

Study 3: 52 real televised appeals for missing relatives; in 26 cases the pleader was later proven to be the killer (murder weapon, security footage, leading police to the body). Across all 52: "There were no instances of UR gazes or UL gazes" (p.4). The behaviour the rule is built on does not occur.

verified Wiseman et al. 2012, PLoS ONE 7(7), Tables 1–2, pp.1–4 · doi, open access

Where "90% accuracy" was born: a 1987 police magazine verified

The figure that sold the technique traces to Rhoads & Solomon (1987) in The Police Chief — a police magazine, not a journal. It mentions four experiments allegedly demonstrating 90%. No reference is given. Nobody ever found them, and no deception study since has come near 90%.

verified cited and dismantled in Wiseman et al. 2012, p.1

The liars DID differ — just not how folklore predicts guardrail

In the same 52 appeals, liars used fewer words, hedged more ("if", "perhaps", "maybe"), blinked more, and their appeals were 41% shorter (10.89 vs 18.37 s, p = .04).

Group averages in one body of footage. Never validated for judging one individual. Nobody should leave this page counting anyone's blinks.

verified Wiseman et al. 2012, p.4, citing ten Brinke & Porter's archive (their own paper is not in our library)

Faces barely carry emotions: the expected face shows up in ~19% of episodes verified

Five researchers from five fields reviewed the whole literature: 89 effect sizes, 47 studies, 3,599 people. When a person actually feels an emotion, the "expected" face (anger → scowl) appears in roughly 19% of episodes (range .15–.25). The reverse question — how often the face appears when the emotion isn't there — is marked unknown for every study in the summary table.

Four days of in-home videography: angry children raised their voices in 42% of anger episodes, whined in 21%, scowled in 16.2%. Anger is audible, not visible.

verified Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019, PSPI 20(1) · doi

Watching Lie to Me made viewers worse, not better abstract-level

Viewers of the show were no more accurate than controls at judging real honest and deceptive interviews — but more likely to misidentify honest people as liars. The show taught suspicion, not detection.

abstract-level Levine, Serota & Shulman 2010, Communication Research 37(6) · doi

What actually works: one cue — detail abstract-level

A 2023 review: humans are barely able to pick up lies from nonverbal cues at all; systematic verbal-content methods do meaningfully better (all still below courtroom certainty).

Nine studies in Nature Human Behaviour: judging freely on any cue = chance. Judging on one cue — how detailed the account is — consistently separated lies from truths. Ignoring more information made people better.

Detail is the through-line of the whole talk: best deception cue, top of the scoring scale, the thing anxiety erodes — and the thing you can train.

abstract-level Brennen & Magnussen 2023, CDPS 32(5) · doi, CC-BY · Verschuere et al. 2023, Nat. Hum. Behav. 7:718–728 · doi

02What actually predicts job performance

The validity ladder · why the textbook was rewritten in 2022 · what "structured" means

What r = .42 is — and is not verified

The experiment behind every number here: interview candidates get scores; months later their job performance is rated (almost always by supervisors); each person is two numbers; the correlation r (−1…+1) says how well one predicts the other; meta-analysis pools this across thousands of people.

.42 is not "42% accuracy" and not "42% of hires succeed." It is a correlation — and .42 vs .19 is the difference between a filter and a ritual.

2022: the field discovers it had been over-correcting for 40 years verified

You can only correlate for the hired — a pre-filtered, narrow group, which shrinks correlations. The field corrected for that legitimately, but the correction's size can only be measured in rare predictive studies, while ~80% of studies are concurrent (current employees, hired years ago, barely restricted). A large correction measured on the 20% was applied to the 80%. The authors: the formulas were "systematically applied in ways which are not appropriate."

Result: IQ .51→.31, work samples .54→.33 — but biodata went up, .35→.38 (so it wasn't a demolition). A JAP reviewer opened with "Why didn't you publish this 40 years ago and save the field a lot of trouble?" Practitioners reported relief: their local numbers had never matched the textbook.

verified Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens 2023, IOP 16:283–300, pp.285–292 · doi, CC-BY

Valid AND fair — the combination once thought impossible verified

Average Black–White score gap: IQ-type test d = .79 (largest in the table); structured interview .23; unstructured .32. The most valid common method is also among the fairest.

verified Sackett et al. 2023, Table 1

The five components you can feel from the chair verified

Same questions for everyone · a written anchored scale (this one component alone: validity .26 → .35) · each answer scored as given · limited probing · trained interviewers. Twelve meta-analyses have compared structured vs unstructured; all twelve favour structured.

And a 2023 experiment: under "act like an applicant" instructions, scores inflated more on traditional questions than structured ones — structure predicts better and is harder to game.

verified Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson & Campion 2014, Personnel Psychology 67, pp.242–274 · doi · abstract-level Bill, Melchers, Steuer & Eisele 2023 · doi

The honest spread: ".42, plus or minus .24" guardrail

SD = .19; printed lower bound of the 80% interval = .18 — the authors' own phrasing: validity "should really be viewed as '.42, plus or minus .24'." The unstructured interview's lower bound is −.01: its worst tenth predicts nothing.

And the field runs on the wrong end: across 104 interviews in the research literature itself, the average used 5.74 of 18 structure components; 4% barred discussing candidates between interviews; 19% took detailed notes; face-to-face interviews were more structured than video ones (6.50 vs 4.28) — the opposite of the same paper's prediction.

You can't control whether the interviewer brings a scale. You control whether your answers are scorable.

verified Sackett p.292 · Levashina Table 2, pp.247–248

03Score two answers — try it yourself

The live exercise from the talk. Vote first, then open the scale.

Question: "Tell me about a time you fixed a broken process."

A. "Oh, that happens all the time in data work. In my last project the whole reporting process was a mess — numbers didn't match, people complained constantly. I basically took charge, cleaned everything up, aligned the stakeholders, and after that the reports were finally reliable. It really changed how the team worked."
B. "Our weekly sales report broke about once a month because an upstream export changed column order without warning. I owned the fix: I wrote a schema check that validates the export before the load and fails loudly instead of loading garbage. It caught three bad files in the next quarter — the log shows each one — and the report hasn't shipped wrong numbers since. The trade-off was a day of delay whenever validation fails, which the team accepted over silent errors."
The interviewer's scale (a BARS):
0 — no relevant example · 1 — relevant situation, but unclear what the person personally did or what came of it · 2 — clear personal action and an observable result · 3 — action and result plus a measurement, a trade-off, or a lesson that survives one more probing question

A scores a 1. Which process? "Reporting." What exactly failed? Not stated. What did you do? "Basically took charge" — a summary of an action, not an action. Not because the person is bad at their job — there is nothing in the answer to evaluate.

B scores a 3. Named failure mode, personal action, a result with an artifact (three catches, in the log) — and the candidate names the cost themselves: a day of delay. Almost nobody does that.

Before the scale, fluency wins. After the scale, evidence wins. The scale doesn't make anyone smarter — it forces everyone to evaluate the same thing. Write answers as if the sheet exists: an answer earning a 3 on an invisible rubric also survives an interviewer running on feel. The reverse is not true.

teaching props both answers written for the talk · scale mechanics: verified anchored scales .26→.35 (Taylor & Small 2002), every-point anchoring beats endpoints (Reilly 2006), BARS makes naive raters match experts (Maurer 2002) — all collected in Levashina 2014 pp.273–274

04What the instrument can't see

The demographic gap that closed · why · personality is barely on the channel

What closed the gap: a clerical habit verified

Not more structure — component counts didn't differ between eras. What differed: post-1996 studies far more often had interviewers score each answer as it was given instead of forming one overall impression at the end (binomial p < .03).

A correlate, not a proven cause — the paper's own strongest candidate. And the claim has edges: this is about ratings, in this set of studies. It says nothing about who got invited to interview.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, Table 3 + pp.254–255

Interviews barely read personality — and a third of questions aim at it verified

Interview ratings vs self-reported traits: conscientiousness .12, extraversion .08, openness .03, agreeableness .01, emotional stability .01. Meanwhile one-third of all questions across 47 real interviews targeted personality.

The honest counterweight: interviewer trait ratings still predict job performance about twice as well as self-reports (.16–.31). Not blind — just far worse than everyone assumes.

Stop rehearsing adjectives ("I'm hardworking"). Rehearse episodes: what you knew, did, and what happened.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, pp.262, 265

05Selling yourself, and what faking actually costs

448 real interviews · the modal lie is flattery · two paths · the real price

The most common lie in an interview is flattery, not a fabricated credential. Interviewers in this study averaged 174 interviews of career experience; candidates reported anonymously after each real interview.

Consistency is not a tell — the fairytale died in the data verified

Across up to fifteen interviews per person, inventing qualifications was the second most stable tactic of seven, nearly matching honest self-promotion. What varied: flattery and defensive explanations. Anyone claiming fabricators "can't keep their story straight" is guessing.

verified Roulin & Bourdage 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 8:29, Tables 2–3 · doi, CC-BY

Faking pulls the rating both ways at once — net negative verified

In 182 videotaped structured interviews, deceptive impression management directly raised interviewer ratings — and simultaneously lowered the answers' plausibility and raised verbal uncertainty, while plausibility itself drives ratings. Two paths, opposite directions. In the largest modern study (693 interviews) the net relation was negative and linear. Honest self-promotion followed an inverted U: helpful, then harmful past a peak nobody has located.

What not to claim: that faking destroys prediction — one study found prediction survived, its authors call the simple story "too simplified."

verified (publisher abstract) Roth, Klehe & Willhardt 2021 · doi, diamond OA · abstract-level Robie et al. 2020 · doi · Buehl et al. 2018 · doi

The real price: it works, and then you live in it verified

206 hired candidates, followed up in two phases: the more deceptive impression management they had used, the worse their person–job and person–organization fit — and fit accounted for lower well-being, lower engagement, and higher job stress. The risk isn't mainly getting caught. The risk is that it works.

And in 202 real interviewees, interview anxiety predicted deceptive impression management — much interview lying is a fear response, not a strategy. Same fix as for nerves.

verified (publisher abstract) Charbonneau, Powell, Spence & Lyons 2021 · doi, CC-BY · abstract-level Powell, Bourdage & Bonaccio 2020 · doi

Charm needs an audience: .27 on the person in the room, .11 on an observer verified

Impression management lifts ratings ≈ .22 overall. When the charmed person also assigns the score: .27. When an outside observer rates the recording: .11, non-significant. Tested mechanism: the engaged interviewer has no spare cognitive capacity to separate charm from content (the "engaged interviewers filter better" hypothesis was rejected). Deliberate detection tops out at 63.4% (coin = 50), and warning candidates doesn't reduce faking — it just sours them on the process.

verified Peck & Levashina 2017, Tables 2 & 6 · doi · abstract-level Roulin & Powell 2018 · doi · Bill & Melchers 2022 · doi

Three honest gaps nobody will tell you about guardrail

The "85–99% of candidates embellish" figure everyone cites is from 2007, a student sample, never re-measured — 2020 papers still cite the 2007 source. There is no modern peer-reviewed evidence on how often CV fraud is discovered or what happens afterwards. There is no peer-reviewed estimate of how many candidates use language models in applications. Anyone quoting a number for these three made it up.

verified Bill, Melchers, Buehl & Wank 2020 · doi, open access

06Building a story that survives probing

No commercial experience needed · six parts · six probes · the uncomfortable finding

No experience, no penalty — the best finding for juniors verified

Having prior job experience does not change how well behavioural questions predict performance. Candidates substitute non-work experience — the review calls this "common in practice." Volunteer, university, open-source and freelance projects are not second-class answers. And the validity paper classifies "tell me about a time" as a work sample: you're not describing yourself, you're showing a sample of work — which can be prepared honestly.

verified Levashina p.268 (Gibb & Taylor 2003) · Sackett p.288

Six parts, six probes — and which parts are actually evidence-based verified

Parts: Context (the constraint that made it hard) · Responsibility (what was yours) · Action · Result (metric, artifact, feedback) · Lesson · Application. Parts 1–4 map onto how these interviews are scored. Parts 5–6 come from a coaching framework with zero validation research — reasonable advice, not evidence, which is why no acronym.

Probes a finished story survives: What did you decide? · What alternative did you reject, and why? · How exactly was the result measured? · What went wrong? · Who disagreed or depended on you? · What artifact exists?

The one habit: answer at least two probes before being asked. Name the failure yourself. Name the measurement yourself. That's the difference between a 2 and a 3.

A probe is a scoring signal, not an attack: debriefed applicants said probing "was perceived as a cue signaling what types of answers were important." (Research irony: probing was expected to inhibit faking — it increased it.)

verified Levashina pp.270–273 · CARLA coaching blog, labelled specimen

More preparation correlates with more faking — kept in on purpose abstract-level

Prep time and coaching correlate with more deceptive — and also more honest — impression management. The reading: the same pressure that drives hard preparation drives the urge to close the gap between what you have and what the posting asked. The move: name the gap out loud ("I haven't done this at production scale; here's the closest thing") instead of papering it over. A named gap is a scorable answer.

abstract-level Schudlik, Reinhard & Müller 2020 · doi

07Your nerves are not the signal you think

2.50 = 2.50 · what people actually fear · specificity, not calm

The full study — predictive, real jobs, two rater sources verified

All 307 took a structured interview + 30-item anxiety questionnaire; 154 were hired; performance was rated 7.5 months later by supervisors and ~17 supervisees each, on live appraisal forms. The interview itself worked (r = .20 with supervisor ratings) — anxiety just wasn't what it picked up. One honest footnote: for anxious candidates the interview stopped predicting one outcome of four (supervisor-rated teamwork).

What people actually fear: highest of five anxiety dimensions — performance anxiety (fear of the verdict), 2.87. Lowest — communication anxiety (fear of talking), 2.18. People fear the verdict, which can't be rehearsed; they least fear the one thing that can be measurably improved.

verified Schneider, Powell & Bonaccio 2019, IJSA 27(4), §2.1, Table 1 · doi, CC-BY

The mechanism: anxiety eats attention → answers lose detail verified

Threat-monitoring consumes the working attention that was building your answer; self-focus cuts disclosure; the measurable output is less detailed answers. So the trainable target is specificity, not calmness. The paper: anxious candidates "may receive lower ratings if they have difficulty articulating a coherent answer in the interview but may communicate clearly once hired."

verified Schneider et al. 2019 §1.3 (mechanism: cited theory — Eysenck 2007, Clark & Wells 1995)

Stop reading your instruments verified

Self-rated performance correlated −.46 with felt anxiety and −.51 with strain — but only ~.39 with what actual raters gave. Your sense of "how it went" measures adrenaline. Heart-rate variability correlated with nothing (max .15); strain peaked during the cognitive test, not the interview.

First impressions: real, related more strongly to early answers, and the effect fades — so small talk is scored territory, a bad start is recoverable, and "80% in 20 seconds" is dead. (Abstract-only source; that's why no effect size is quoted.)

verified Melchers, Petrig, Basch & Sauer 2021, Tables 1 & 3 · doi · abstract-level Swider, Barrick & Harris 2016 · PubMed

08Phone, video, and the machine

The video penalty · reps beat brains · what AI actually reads · hardest to fool

"No difference" vs "half a standard deviation" — resolved verified

A 2026 meta-analysis (24 studies / 31 samples / 3,874 people overall) found no rating difference remote vs in-person: ρ = −.01. Precision matters: the ratings comparison rests on 7 samples, 898 people, and the authors write it would be "imprudent to draw any conclusion at this point."

A randomized experiment (N=88, same structured interview): video d = 0.49 below face-to-face; the (now-museum) telephone arm was even worse, d = 0.89 — the thinner the channel, the bigger the penalty. The "contradiction" dissolves in the meta's own coding: it kept only the video arm of that experiment and discarded the telephone arm — the largest effect in the study.

verified Moon, Park & Park 2026, Table 2 · doi, open · Melchers et al. 2021, Table 2 · doi

What survives: the monologue travels, reps beat brains verified

The prepared self-introduction showed no medium effect at all (4.21 / 4.08 / 4.13, F<1) — the interactive questions absorbed the entire penalty. It's not stress: anxiety, strain and heart rate didn't differ between media. So train the interaction, not the pitch.

And: prior interview count predicted scores (.30/.23/.22) while the 50-item cognitive test predicted nothing significant. The least romantic advice in the library — do more mock interviews — has the strongest support.

Candidate reactions: organizational attractiveness drops after remote (−.17); intention to pursue drops for asynchronous interviews (−.20) but not live video (+.03) — with async and AI-scored perfectly confounded (identical eight samples), so nobody can yet separate them.

verified Melchers et al. 2021, Tables 1–2 · Moon et al. 2026, Table 2 + p.14

What an AI interview system actually reads — a vendor's own words verified

A major vendor's five-page product document (Dec 2025): your answer is transcribed, the transcript is "processed using LMs," candidates are "shown in tiers and stack-ranked." A keyword sweep of all five pages: face, facial, visual, video, tone, voice, vocal, expression, emotion, body, gesture, appearance, smile, eye — zero occurrences. Every scored signal is text.

Sharper still: the model is trained "to predict an expert evaluator's rating" — not job performance. Four criterion substitutions, and no validity coefficient anywhere in the document. The ceiling of such a system is the human rater it imitates. Not an objective instrument — a subjective one, at scale. (Scope: one vendor, one document, one date.)

verified HireVue product document, 5 pp., reproducible keyword sweep

Machines are harder to fool — the finding nobody guesses abstract-level

Inflation by channel (chart above): self-report .62, humans .22, AI .14. A field study of 144 real candidate–interviewer pairs found async video produced less deceptive behaviour than live; merely telling people their answers would be machine-scored reduced deceptive impression management. One dissent measured intentions (not behaviour) rising — unresolved tension, not a contradiction.

ChatGPT-written answers (preregistered, N=245): higher content scores, unchanged delivery, lower honesty ratings — raters marked something down without naming it — and those candidates rated the process as less fair.

abstract-level Hickman et al. 2025 · doi · Suen & Hung 2023 · doi · Langer et al. 2020 · doi · Agnihotri & Bhattacharya 2025 · doi · Canagasuriam & Lukacik 2024 · doi

09What EU law gives you

Two rights, one date — and what's already in force not legal advice

Right 1: they have to tell you · Right 2: you can ask why verified

Art 26(11): where a high-risk system makes — or helps make — a decision about a person, the deployer must inform them. A human clicking "accept" on a machine-sorted shortlist does not switch the duty off. Recital 93: the information includes purpose, decision types, and your right to an explanation.

Art 86: the right to "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken." Not the source code — an account of what the machine did and what the decision turned on.

Recruitment is named high-risk in so many words (Annex III 4(a)), and any system that profiles people is "always considered to be high-risk" (Art 6(3)) — covering essentially every candidate-scoring tool.

verified Reg. (EU) 2024/1689, pp.54, 69, 110, 127 · postponement to 2 Dec 2027: Reg. (EU) 2026/1744, p.35

The emotion-inference ban — satisfying and deflating verified

Since 2 Feb 2025, inferring emotions at the workplace via AI is prohibited (Art 5(1)(f)). The satisfying part: the recital cites "serious concerns about the scientific basis" — naming "the limited reliability, the lack of specificity and the limited generalisability." The law read the same science as section 01. The deflating part: the ban bites only via biometric data (Art 3(39)). A transcript-only pipeline is outside it entirely — which is exactly what the vendor in section 08 runs. Fines for prohibition breaches: up to €35m or 7% of turnover.

What you have today, no AI required verified

EU-wide, public and private sector: discrimination in recruitment is banned on grounds including age, sex, disability, ethnic or racial origin, religion or belief, and sexual orientation. Reasonable accommodation expressly covers the application stage — "from the job application process to termination." Every EU country has a national equality body: it explains, helps you complain, and some will take your case to court with your approval. Remedies: compensation, reinstatement, or an order to fix and accommodate. Asking costs nothing.

verified Your Europe — Equal treatment in recruitment (official EU page)

Genuinely unsettled guardrail

Whether a job interview counts as "the workplace" for the emotion ban — the Act never defines workplace and never mentions applicants in the prohibition. And whether the explanation right has practical bite before Dec 2027 (it applies from Aug 2026, but high-risk classification rules apply 16 months later). The Commission owes guidelines that would settle both. Until then: "unresolved," not a confident answer.

10Poland

The 26 questions · the trap · six questions to ask them

The trap, the frame, and your local rights verified

The trap: the state careers portal lists "how do you assess your previous boss" as a common question — and lists badmouthing a previous employer as deadly sin #2. Decide your answer in advance.

The frame: two of the portal's three stated employer purposes are verification (documents, CV truth), not prediction. Bring paper copies; expect the CV to be checked against your answers.

Improper questions (the portal's own list): marital status, number of children, maternity plans, sexual orientation, religion; criminal record only where specific provisions allow.

verified zielonalinia.gov.pl, "Rozmowa kwalifikacyjna — jak się przygotować?" — the question typology (4/3/19) is our coding of their list

Six questions to ask them — plus what the list omits verified

Role priorities · onboarding and who owns it · how results are actually assessed · what determines pay and how to earn more · who you'll work with · promotion prospects. Never skip the bold two — the answer tells you whether they know.

What the list omits, and Poland-critical: contract type (umowa o pracę / zlecenie / B2B), probation, working time, remote policy, notice period. inference — our addendum.

10BThe instrument was built in America. You weren't.

Modesty reads as missing data · what cross-cultural research actually says

The mismatch, stated mechanically inference

The scoring rubric wants personal action: a 2 requires "clear personal action and an observable result." A candidate raised on "we did it" etiquette — where naming your own contribution feels like boasting, and where the Polish portal literally lists "don't boast" among its commandments — answers collectively, and the rubric has nothing to attach a score to. Modesty doesn't read as modesty through this instrument. It reads as missing data. (The mechanics are verified; this cultural consequence is our inference, labelled.)

The 10-country study — folk wisdom loses again abstract-level

N = 3,509 across 10 countries, Russia included: cultures differ in which self-presentation tactics candidates endorse — most on "individual excellence" — and self-presentation carried more weight in hierarchical, high-embeddedness cultures, with the American sample as the exception. So "post-Soviet candidates undersell, Americans oversell" is folklore too; what differs is which tactics feel appropriate.

Honest gap: no study measures how CIS-raised candidates actually score in Western-style interviews. We claim the mechanism and the documented differences — not a penalty coefficient nobody has published.

abstract-level Sandal, van de Vijver, Bye et al. 2014, JCCP 45(6) · doi, Green OA · companion: Bye et al. 2011 · doi

The translation layer — three rules, no personality transplant practice

1. "I" for actions, "we" for credit. "The team shipped the migration — my part was the schema validation and the cutover plan." That sentence structure is the entire skill.

2. Precision instead of praise. You never have to say you're excellent — numbers and artifacts are culturally neutral; adjectives aren't.

3. Name the register switch. "In my professional culture we credit the team, so let me be specific about my own part" — itself a demonstration of self-awareness.

What we could not verify — and what we got wrong

Kept deliberately. Better you have the gaps than a clean story.

Marked unverifiable (selection of 17)
  • The four experiments behind "90% accuracy" — referenced in 1987, never found.
  • The 85–99% embellishment rate — one 2007 student sample, never re-measured.
  • Base rates and consequences of CV fraud — no post-2017 peer-reviewed evidence.
  • Prevalence of LLM use in real applications — no peer-reviewed estimate exists.
  • Swider et al. 2016 (first impressions) — abstract only; effect sizes not quoted anywhere in our materials.
  • Ho, Powell & Stanley 2021 — a meta-analysis on our exact question, closed access; acknowledged, carries no numbers here.
  • Powell, Stanley & Brown 2018 (anxiety↔performance r = −.19) — cited inside a source, original not in the library.
  • Suen & Hung 2024 figures — a 2025 corrigendum we have not read; quarantined.
  • Polish Labour Code article numbers — deliberately excluded until verified.
  • Whether a job interview is "the workplace" under Art 5(1)(f) — unresolved in the legal texts themselves.
Corrections we made to our own drafts (selection of 9)
  • "Fabrication is inconsistent between interviews" — flatly contradicted by the data; removed and guarded.
  • "31 samples / 3,874 people → ρ = −.01" — conflated the whole meta with its ratings subset (7 samples / 898); fixed everywhere.
  • The EU emotion ban read as covering all AI interview scoring — it bites only via biometric data; transcript pipelines are outside it.
  • Async vs AI-scored presented as two findings — they are the identical eight samples; presented as one confounded finding.
  • An early draft implied observer raters are "objective" — the effect merely didn't reach significance in a smaller sample.
