Verification status — please read before quoting anything

The 18 core sources were read in full and every statistic was checked against the source file, at page and table level.

The 18 newer sources (2018–2026) on faking and AI interviews were located through bibliographic databases, and most of their statistics come from published abstracts rather than full text — several publishers blocked the article bodies. The numbers are real and the DOIs resolve, but they have not had the same page-level check. They are marked abstract-level throughout.

If you are going to rely on one of those figures for something that matters, read the paper.

Section 1Can anyone tell when you're lying?

No. And the specific technique most people believe in has been tested and failed three times in one paper.

16.33 vs 16.59

Correct lie-detection judgements out of 32, for people trained in the "liars look up and to the right" rule versus untrained controls. Chance is 16. The trained group did nominally worse. Training also did not increase their confidence (4.65 vs 4.58 on a 7-point scale) — so the rule doesn't work, and it doesn't feel like it fails.

verified Wiseman et al. 2012, PLoS ONE 7(7):e40259, Study 2, Table 2, p.4 · open access

Zero

Instances of sustained upward eye movement, in either direction, across 52 real televised appeals for missing relatives — 26 of them by people later proven to have been lying. Not "no difference between liars and truth-tellers": the behaviour the technique is built on did not occur at all. Ground truth was established by evidence such as possession of the murder weapon, security footage, or the person leading police to the body.

verified Wiseman et al. 2012, Study 3, pp.4–5 · open access

Where the "90% accuracy" claim comes from

A 1987 article in a police magazine referred to four experiments demonstrating 90% accuracy and gave no reference for any of them. Nobody has located them since, and no deception-detection study has ever reached that accuracy. The paper's authors note that promoting decisions on this basis "would seem irresponsible."

The claim is also a mutation. The originators of neuro-linguistic programming did not treat "constructed" mental imagery as lying; that reading came later.

verified Wiseman et al. 2012, Introduction p.1 and General Discussion p.5

The cues that did differ — and why you still shouldn't use them

In the same 52 videos, liars used fewer words, more tentative language ("if", "perhaps", "maybe"), blinked more, and their appeals were about 41% shorter (10.9 seconds versus 18.4).

guardrail These are group-level averages across one body of footage. They were never validated as rules for judging an individual, and using them that way is how the nose-touching myth started. The defensible takeaway points the other way: in your own answers, cut the hedging and commit to concrete detail.

cited within Wiseman et al. Original coding: ten Brinke & Porter

The bigger claim: can faces be read at all?

≈19%

How often the expected facial configuration actually appeared when someone was experiencing the relevant emotion — across 47 studies and 3,599 participants. The mean correlation was r = .32. And these are an upper bound: matching whole facial ensembles rather than partial configurations produced lower values still.

verified Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 20(1) · free to read an aggregate — not "a scowl means anger 19% of the time"

Unknown

What the review's own summary table reports for specificity in every single production study — that is, how often the "angry face" appears when the person isn't angry. Not tested and failed: never reported, because studies don't publish false-positive rates. So we don't know what a scowl rules out.

verified Barrett et al. 2019, Table 4

More from the same review — the vivid findings

Angry children are audible, not visible. Four days of in-home videography: children raised their voices in 42% of anger situations, whined in 21%, and scowled in 16.2%.

Winning doesn't make you smile — an audience does. Only 8 of 55 winning judo fighters produced a genuine (Duchenne) smile in one study, and all 8 occurred during social interaction; 25 of 119 in a second study. Olympic medallists smiled facing the crowd, not facing the flag.

Infants make the "surprise" face for mechanical reasons. Opening the mouth necessarily raises the eyebrows — and they don't reliably make it when actually surprised. Arm-restrained one-year-olds produced the proposed anger configuration 24% of the time, while 54% produced the surprise configuration.

Congenitally blind people undercut the innateness argument. Their spontaneous facial movements resemble sighted people's — both non-diagnostic. But they pose the stereotyped expressions less reliably. Learning to perform the expressions appears to depend on vision.

The multiple-choice format manufactures universality. The method used to demonstrate universal expressions produces the same result for invented emotion categories with made-up cues, via a process of elimination.

Every pre-2008 universality claim rested on three papers — two of them peer-reviewed — reporting four samples.

Mining 7 million images found 17 distinct facial configurations for "happiness", five for anger, one for disgust — and none matched the configurations Darwin or prior research proposed.

be fair to the other side The paper states it is not a referendum on any researcher's view, criticises its own authors' earlier work, and quotes opposing researchers agreeing there is no one-to-one correspondence between facial movements and emotions. The dispute is about how much variation exists and why — not whether it exists.

verified Barrett et al. 2019, throughout

An honest limit on how we used this

This review never mentions hiring. Zero occurrences of hiring, recruitment, employment or applicant; the domains it names are law, national security, psychiatric diagnosis, autism, education and commercial products. Extending it to interviews is our argument, not theirs. The authors' own carve-out is precise: they object to inferring an internal emotional state, not to detecting movement for purposes like pain or drowsiness detection.

Watching people who "read faces" — and what actually works

The TV show that taught a generation to read microexpressions has itself been tested. Participants watched Lie to Me, then judged real honest and deceptive interviews. Viewers were no more accurate than controls — but more likely to misidentify honest people as liars. The show taught suspicion, not detection.

abstract-level Levine, Serota & Shulman 2010, Communication Research 37(6) · doi

A 2023 review of the whole lie-detection field concludes that humans are barely able to pick up lies from nonverbal cues at all; systematic methods analysing what is said do meaningfully better — and even those stay well below courtroom certainty.

abstract-level Brennen & Magnussen 2023, Current Directions in Psychological Science 32(5) · open access, CC-BY

And a nine-study paper in Nature Human Behaviour: when people judged statements intuitively, free to use any cue, they performed at chance. Instructed to ignore everything except how detailed the account is, they consistently told lies from truths. Ignoring more information made them better — the signal is in the specificity of what is said, not in the face.

abstract-level Verschuere et al. 2023, Nature Human Behaviour 7:718–728 · doi · PMID 36941469

A correlation is not a percentage

.42 does not mean 42% accurate, and it does not mean a 42% chance of hiring the right person. It measures how tightly two things move together.

.42 ± .24

The authors' own framing of structured-interview validity. The standard deviation is .19 and the printed lower bound is .18 — so a badly-built structured interview is a worse instrument than a well-built anything else. For unstructured interviews the bottom of the range is about zero (−.01).

verified Sackett et al. 2023, Table 1 and p.292

.79 vs .23

Black–White score difference (Cohen's d) for cognitive-ability tests versus structured interviews. The cognitive test has the largest group difference in the whole table; the structured interview is among the smallest. So the most accurate common instrument is also one of the fairest — which was not assumed to be possible.

verified Sackett et al. 2023, Table 1 and Figure 1, pp.285–286

The 40-year error, explained

To know whether a test predicts job performance you need people who took the test and have a performance record — which means people you hired. But hired people are more similar to each other than applicants are, because the extremes were filtered out. Narrow range shrinks correlations, so the raw figure understates the truth. The field corrected for this for decades.

To size that correction you need to know how much wider the applicant pool was. You only know that in predictive studies — test the applicants, wait, measure performance. Those show large narrowing. But about 80% of studies in these meta-analyses were concurrent: test current employees today, rate them today. Those people were hired years earlier on entirely different grounds, so their range on the test barely narrowed at all.

The field took the large correction, derived from the 20%, and applied it to the 80%. The authors are explicit that the formulas were never wrong — only where they were pointed.

Effects: work samples fell .54 → .33, cognitive ability .51 → .31, unstructured interviews .38 → .19. Only two predictors went up: vocational interests and empirically keyed biodata. In 21st-century data cognitive ability falls further, to .23 across 113 studies.

Composite validity barely moved (.47 versus .51 under the old figures), so selection practice was not undermined — but the ranking changed, and with it the case for building systems around cognitive tests. Removing the cognitive test from a six-method battery now costs about .05, where it used to cost .20.

verified Sackett et al. 2023, pp.284–291

What "structured" actually means — the 15 components

Content: questions based on job analysis · the same questions for every candidate · limited prompting and follow-up · better question types · a longer interview with more questions · controlling ancillary information · no candidate questions until the end.

Evaluation: rating each answer rather than an overall impression · anchored rating scales · detailed notes · multiple interviewers · the same interviewers across candidates · no discussion between interviews · interviewer training · combining scores statistically rather than by discussion.

Anchored rating scales are the best-evidenced single component: validity of .35 with them versus .26 without, and inter-rater reliability .77 versus .73. Anchoring every scale point beats anchoring only the extremes.

An awkward finding: across 104 interviews described in the research literature, the average used 5.74 of 18 components. Only 4% prohibited discussion between interviews; 19% took detailed notes; 11% combined scores statistically. And face-to-face interviews were more structured (6.50 components) than video ones (4.28) — the opposite of what theory predicts.

verified Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson & Campion 2014, Personnel Psychology 67:241–293, pp.244–249, Tables 1–2, p.273 · paywalled: doi

Question types: situational versus past-behaviour

Uncorrected validity, from three meta-analyses: situational questions .25–.35, past-behaviour questions .31. these are uncorrected and not comparable to the .42 above

Inter-rater reliability: situational .79, past-behaviour .76.

They measure different things. Situational questions predict typical performance at .41 and maximum performance at .25. Past-behaviour questions predict typical performance at .34 but maximum performance at only .11 (not significant). So hypothetical questions probe your ceiling; "tell me about a time" probes your normal day.

Situational questions map onto job knowledge; past-behaviour questions onto experience. Job-complexity moderation is inconsistent across two meta-analyses, and incremental validity findings contradict each other.

Predictive studies show validity about .10 lower than concurrent studies for both types.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, pp.267–268 the underlying meta-analyses are cited within

Section 3Fairness — and the mechanism nobody expected

d = −.02

Average demographic difference in structured-interview ratings across post-1996 studies — 10 studies, roughly 121,000 people, 95% CI [−.06, .01]. Statistically indistinguishable from zero, and slightly favouring minority and female candidates. Before 1996 the same figure was d = .20.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, Table 3, p.254 no average difference detected in ratings, in this evidence set — not "bias is solved", and it says nothing about who was invited to interview

Why it changed

Not more structure — the number of structure components used was essentially the same in both sets of studies. What differed: post-1996 studies were significantly more likely to have interviewers score each answer as it was given, rather than forming an overall impression at the end.

A clerical habit, not a values programme. verified · binomial test, p < .03

What interviews fail to measure

Correlations between interview ratings and self-reported personality: conscientiousness .12, extraversion .08, openness .03, agreeableness .01, emotional stability .01.

Meanwhile, across 47 real interviews, about a third of all questions were aimed at personality.

the counterweight Interviewer-rated personality still predicts job performance roughly twice as well as self-reported personality. So interviewers are not useless at reading people — just far worse at it than the process assumes.

Practical consequence: rehearsing "I'm a hard worker" is spending time on the channel the instrument barely reads. Rehearse knowledge and concrete past behaviour instead.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, pp.262–265 underlying meta-analyses cited within

Section 4Impression management, and what faking costs

What follows treats self-presentation as a normal feature of hiring rather than a moral failing, because that is how the evidence treats it.

4.02 vs 1.38

Self-reported use of honest self-promotion versus inventing qualifications, on a 1–5 scale, across 448 real placement interviews by 80 candidates with professional interviewers who had conducted an average of 175 interviews each.

Full ranking: honest self-promotion 4.02 · honest ingratiation 3.53 · deceptive ingratiation 2.99 · honest defensive explanations 2.77 · embellishing real qualifications 2.04 · hiding negatives 1.54 · inventing qualifications 1.38.

verified Roulin & Bourdage 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 8:29, Table 2, p.7 · open access, CC BY

The finding most people get wrong

The most common form of dishonesty in a job interview is not a fabricated credential. It is insincere flattery — deceptive ingratiation is the third most-used tactic of the seven, above honest defensive explanations.

Who fakes — and the correction we had to make

An earlier draft of this talk attached the more dramatic column to "fabrication." That was wrong. Embellishing what is real and inventing what isn't are two different behaviours with two different profiles.

essential These are correlations from one study of 80 people reporting on themselves. They describe group-level tendencies and cannot be reversed to diagnose an individual.

The reassuring half: honest self-promotion is essentially uncorrelated with every dark trait (psychopathy .03, Machiavellianism −.03) and positively related to extraversion and self-confidence. It is also the most-used behaviour of the seven. Selling yourself honestly is the norm, not a warning sign.

Two more oddities: self-monitoring — the "good actor" trait — did not predict faking at all; it predicted only switching tactics between interviews. And students further into their degree faked less, not more.

verified Roulin & Bourdage 2017, Table 2 and p.6

A claim we removed from this talk

We wanted to tell you that fabricated stories fall apart because they are inconsistent — that people who invent things change their story between interviews.

The study measured exactly that, across up to 15 interviews per person. Inventing qualifications was the second-most-stable behaviour of all seven, almost as consistent as honest self-promotion. What people actually varied was flattery and excuses.

So consistency is not the tell. If you hear someone make that claim, they are guessing. verified · coefficient-of-variation intervals, p.6

So what does faking actually do?

Two paths

Deceptive impression management has a positive direct effect on interviewer ratings — and a negative indirect effect, because it reduces the plausibility of your answers and increases verbal uncertainty. Plausibility is itself a predictor of ratings. This explains why older studies appeared to contradict each other: they measured different paths.

abstract-level Roth, Klehe & Willhardt 2021, Personnel Assessment and Decisions 7(1), N = 182 · open access

Net negative

In the largest modern study — 693 high-fidelity interviews — deceptive impression management related linearly and negatively to interview performance. The same study found honest impression management follows an inverted U: effective up to a point, then detrimental.

abstract-level Robie, Christiansen, Bourdage, Powell & Roulin 2020, IJSA 28(4):522–530 · doi (paywalled)

206 hired

People who used more deceptive impression management in their interviews and were then hired reported worse fit with the job and organisation — and worse fit predicted lower well-being, lower engagement and higher job stress.

So the modern answer to "what happens if I fake it" is not mainly that you get caught. It is that you succeed, and end up in a role designed for the person you described.

abstract-level Charbonneau, Powell, Spence & Lyons 2021, Personnel Assessment and Decisions 7(1), two-phase field study · open access

63.4%

The best accuracy anyone has achieved detecting interview faking through systematic content analysis. On a two-way judgement where guessing scores 50%. Multimodal machine-learning approaches perform "above chance" but their usefulness is described as questionable; a thermal-imaging study found essentially nothing.

And faking warnings do not work: telling candidates you use lie-detection methods reduced neither faking intentions nor faking, and made candidates feel worse about the process.

abstract-level Roulin & Powell 2018, Journal of Personnel Psychology 17(3) · warnings: Bill & Melchers 2022, IJSA 31(1)

Faking is often fear, not strategy

In 202 real interviewees, interview anxiety was positively related to deceptive impression management. Honesty-humility and extraversion related to faking indirectly, via anxiety.

Which reframes the whole topic: a good deal of interview dishonesty looks like a fear response under time pressure rather than calculated advantage-seeking. It also means the intervention is the same as the one for nerves.

abstract-level Powell, Bourdage & Bonaccio 2020, Journal of Business and Psychology 36(5):829–840

Charm works on people, not on processes

Across 32 studies (n = 3,792), impression management raised interview ratings by rc = .22. Self-focused tactics .24; other-focused .17.

But split by who scores: .27 when the person exposed to the behaviour also rated the candidate, versus .11 and non-significant when an uninvolved observer rated a recording.

The mechanism is cognitive load, not carelessness. Researchers specifically tested whether the engaged interviewer filters impression management better — and found the opposite.

note This does not make observers objective. It means impression-management contamination was not detectable in their ratings in that smaller subset.

Also worth knowing: real job-seekers convert impression management into more than double the rating benefit students do (.36 vs .15) at the same frequency of use — which suggests it is a learned skill, and that student-sample research understates it.

verified Peck & Levashina 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 8:201, Tables 2, 6, 8 · open access, CC BY

Three things nobody has measured

1. The figure everyone quotes — that 85–99% of candidates embellish — is from 2007, from a student sample, and has never been re-measured. Papers published in 2020 still cite that 2007 source.

2. There is no post-2017 peer-reviewed evidence on how often CV misrepresentation is discovered, or what happens to people when it is.

3. There is no peer-reviewed estimate of how many people use language models to write real job applications.

Anyone quoting you a number for these is guessing.

Section 5Phone, video, asynchronous, AI

Two studies that looked like a contradiction

A 2026 meta-analysis (24 studies, 31 samples, 3,874 people in total) found no average difference in interview ratings between technology-mediated and face-to-face interviews: ρ = −.01, 95% CI [−.17, .16]. To be exact about what that null rests on: the ratings comparison itself draws on 7 samples and 898 people — the larger figures describe the whole meta-analysis across all outcomes.

A randomised experiment (N = 88, identical structured interview) found candidates scored d = 0.89 lower by telephone and 0.49 lower on video.

The resolution: when the meta-analysis coded that experiment it kept only the video arm and discarded the telephone arm — where the largest effect was. For fairness it used the experiment's post-interview measure (no difference) rather than the large pre-interview one. So the two largely agree, and what looked like a contradiction was a coding decision.

The meta-analysis is candid about the rating estimate: "It would be imprudent to draw any conclusion at this point." Only 18% of its variance was attributable to artifacts.

verified Moon, Park & Park 2026, Current Psychology 45:349 (open access) · Melchers, Petrig, Basch & Sauer 2021, Frontiers in Psychology 11:603632 (open access)

Monologue: immune

In that experiment the prepared self-introduction showed no medium effect (d = .21 and .13, not significant) while the interactive structured questions took the entire hit. The authors' explanation: structured questions demand continuous real-time adjustment; the introduction is a monologue.

Practical reading: rehearsed material travels through any medium. Improvised interaction may not — so rehearse the interaction.

verified Melchers et al. 2021, Table 2, p.10 the practical instruction is our inference

Not stress

The same experiment found no differences between media in interview anxiety, self-reported strain, or heart-rate variability. So the remote performance gap is not a nerves effect. Separately, heart-rate variability correlated with no performance measure (maximum .15), and measured strain peaked during the cognitive-ability test rather than the interview.

verified Melchers et al. 2021, Tables 1–3

Reps beat brains

Prior interview experience predicted ratings (.30, .23, .22). The 50-item cognitive-ability test administered in the same study did not significantly predict interview performance. The strongest argument available for mock interviews over more reading.

verified Melchers et al. 2021, Table 1, p.9

Candidates mispredict how bad remote interviews feel

Before the interview, candidates rated telephone and video formats far less favourably than face-to-face (d up to 1.38). Afterwards, fairness differences were gone — because the remote conditions rose (telephone d = 0.70, video d = 1.14) while face-to-face stayed flat.

Across formats generally, candidates rate the employer lower (organisational attractiveness ρ = −.17). But intention to pursue the job falls specifically for asynchronous formats (−.20) while live video is neutral (+.03).

important confound In that evidence, asynchronous and AI-scored are perfectly confounded — literally the same eight samples. Nobody can currently separate "recording alone" from "scored by a machine."

Also: telephone and videoconference did not differ from each other on anything, which contradicts what media-richness and social-presence theories predict.

verified Melchers et al. 2021 · Moon et al. 2026, Table 2

What AI interview systems actually score

Transcripts

One major vendor's own AI product document (5 pages, generated 19 December 2025) describes its AI-scored interview pipeline as: recorded response → transcript → language model → predicted competency rating → candidates "shown in tiers and stack-ranked."

Searching that document for face, facial, visual, video, tone, voice, vocal, expression, emotion, body, gesture, appearance, smile and eye returns zero occurrences of any of them. Every scored signal described is text.

verified HireVue, "AI in Hirevue Products", p.2 and keyword sweep of all five pages one vendor, one document, one date — other products and earlier versions may differ, and absence from a document is not proof about an industry

The criterion problem

What is that model predicting? Not job performance. It is trained, in the document's own words, to predict an expert evaluator's rating. The game-based assessment predicts your score on a different psychometric test. The assessment builder was trained on ratings generated by a language model and reviewed by psychologists.

Nowhere in five pages is the target a work outcome, and no coefficient is given for any link in any chain — no validity figure, no adverse-impact ratio, no independent bias audit. The word "fair" appears six times and is never operationalised; "objective" is claimed for the two products that use no AI at all.

verified HireVue product document, pp.2–4 and keyword counts the "imitation of a subjective instrument" reading is ours

The finding that surprises everyone

Machines appear to be harder to fake than humans, not easier.

152 people did the same asynchronous interview twice — once instructed to answer honestly, once to answer as a candidate trying to get hired. Score inflation: self-reported personality d = 0.62, human interviewer ratings 0.22, AI ratings 0.14. The AI was the least fakeable channel.

It replicates in the field. A study of 144 real interviewee–interviewer pairs found asynchronous video interviews produced fewer deceptive behaviours than live video, with AI-assisted assessment specifically reducing outright invention. And a separate experiment found that simply telling people their answers would be automatically evaluated reduced deceptive impression management.

unresolved tension One paper points the other way — it found AI interviewers increase the intention to fake. But it measured intentions; the three above measured behaviour.

abstract-level Hickman, Liff, Willis & Kim 2025, IJSA 33(2) · Suen & Hung 2023, IT & People 38(2) · Langer, König & Hemsing 2020, JMP 35(4)

Using ChatGPT to write your answers

One preregistered experiment, N = 245, three conditions: answers written unaided, answers taken verbatim from ChatGPT, and ChatGPT answers personalised by the candidate.

The ChatGPT conditions scored considerably higher on overall performance and content. Delivery ratings did not differ. But they received lower honesty ratings — and those candidates rated the process as less procedurally fair than the people who wrote their own.

one study This is the first and principal study of its kind. And there is no peer-reviewed estimate of how common the practice actually is, so any percentage you see quoted is not a research finding.

abstract-level Canagasuriam & Lukacik 2024, IJSA 33(1) · doi

Section 6Anxiety

2.50 = 2.50

Mean interview anxiety of candidates who were hired versus those who were not, across 307 real high-stakes interviews (154 hired). t(305) = 0.049, p = .96. Identical to two decimal places.

verified Schneider, Powell & Bonaccio 2019, IJSA 27(4):328–336, §2.1 · open access, CC BY 4.0

.02 to .07

Correlation between interview anxiety and job performance measured 7.5 months later — by supervisors and by an average of 17 people each participant supervised. All four correlations non-significant, and every one positive: the expected negative relationship was not even directionally present. The interview itself did predict performance (r = .20, p = .01).

verified Schneider et al. 2019, Table 1 and Results one job type, one sample; and one moderation effect did appear — in 1 of 4 outcomes

The mechanism, and therefore the fix

Anxiety does not cost you points by making you look nervous. It works through attention: threat-monitoring competes with the task of constructing an answer, and self-focus reduces disclosure. The measurable output is less detailed answers.

So the trainable target is specificity, not calmness. "Relax" is unusable advice; "your answers get thinner under pressure, so over-prepare the detail" is actionable.

The paper's own line: anxious candidates may receive lower ratings if they struggle to articulate a coherent answer, but may communicate clearly once hired.

What people are actually anxious about: of five measured dimensions, the highest was fear of the outcome (2.87) and the lowest was anxiety about communicating (2.18). People fear the verdict, not the talking.

Stop reading your own instruments. Self-rated interview performance correlated −.46 with how anxious people felt and −.51 with strain — but only about .39 with what raters actually gave. Your sense of how it went measures your adrenaline.

cited only A prior meta-analysis puts the anxiety→interview-rating relationship at about r = −.19. We could not obtain that meta-analysis.

verified Schneider et al. 2019, §1.3 and Table 1 · self-rating figures: Melchers et al. 2021, Table 1

First impressions

Impressions formed during the opening minutes relate more strongly to how early answers are scored than later ones, and part of that influence overlaps with genuinely job-related content — so it is not purely bias. Two implications: the interview starts at hello, and the effect fades, so a weak start is recoverable.

no effect size available Our copy of this study is the abstract only. N = 163 mock interviews is the only recoverable number, and we will not invent one. The "first 20 seconds decide 80%" claim circulating online has no support anywhere.

Swider, Barrick & Harris 2016, Journal of Applied Psychology 101(5):625–638 · PubMed (paywalled)

Section 7Building answers when you don't have commercial experience

No penalty

Prior job experience does not moderate the validity of past-behaviour questions. Candidates without work experience substitute non-work experience — and the review notes that doing so "is common in practice."

So a volunteer project, a university project, an open-source contribution or a freelance job is not a second-class answer. What matters is naming the context accurately and owning the specifics.

verified Levashina et al. 2014, p.268 original study cited within

The six steps, and the six probes

Structure: Context — the problem and the constraint that made it hard. Responsibility — what was specifically yours. Action — what you decided and did. Result — a metric, artifact, feedback or visible change. Lesson — what changed in how you think. Application — why this role should care.

craft, not evidence The first four map onto how these interviews are scored. The last two come from a career-coaching framework with no validation study behind it. Useful; not evidence.

The six probes a story must survive: What did you personally decide? What alternative did you reject, and why? How exactly was the result measured? What failed? Who disagreed with you, or depended on you? Which artifact exists because of your work?

Answer at least two before being asked. Volunteering the thing that failed, or how you measured it, is the difference between a mid and a top score against an anchored scale.

A probe is a scoring signal, not an attack. Debriefed candidates reported that probing told them what the interviewer thought mattered. (Which is also why probing has been found to increase faking — probes cue the desired answer.)

verified Levashina et al. 2014, pp.270–273 · framework: Evans, "CARLA", launchedbylinda.com (career-coaching blog, no evidence cited)

An uncomfortable finding about preparation

Two studies found that more preparation time correlated with more faking — and that preparing via online videos or paid interview coaching correlated with more deceptive impression management as well as more honest impression management.

We don't think preparing makes people dishonest. The likelier reading is that the same pressure that drives hard preparation drives the urge to close the gap between what you have and what the advert asked for. The better response is to name the gap out loud in the room rather than paper over it.

abstract-level Schudlik, Reinhard & Müller 2020, IJSA · the interpretation is ours

Section 8Your rights in the EU

Not legal advice

General orientation only, based on reading the official texts. If you have an actual case, get actual advice.

Already in force

Discrimination in recruitment is banned across the EU on grounds including age, sex, disability, ethnic or racial origin, religion or belief, and sexual orientation — public and private sector, covering recruitment, working conditions, promotion, pay, training, pensions and dismissal.

If you have a disability you can request reasonable adjustments — and this expressly covers the job application process, not only employment after hiring.

How to use it: every EU country has a national equality body. They will explain your situation, help you make a complaint, and with your agreement some will take the case to court on your behalf. Trade unions, NGOs and lawyers are named alternatives. Remedies, depending on national law, include compensation, being placed in the job, or an order requiring the employer to remedy the discrimination and provide the adjustment.

AI in recruitment

What the AI Act gives you, and when

Emotion inference is prohibited in workplace and education settings, except for medical or safety reasons — in force since 2 February 2025. But note the scope: it applies to inference from biometric data. A transcript-only system is outside it. And the boundary is precise — merely detecting a smile is out of scope; converting that smile into an inferred emotion is in.

The law's own reasoning cites the science directly, naming "the limited reliability, the lack of specificity and the limited generalisability" of these systems, and the imbalance of power at work.

Recruitment AI is high-risk. Systems for recruitment or selection — placing targeted job ads, filtering applications, evaluating candidates — are listed high-risk uses. There is a narrow exemption for systems that pose no significant risk, but any system that profiles you "shall always be considered to be high-risk," which covers essentially every candidate-scoring tool. that last clause is our inference from the profiling provision

You must be told. Where a high-risk system makes or assists in making decisions about you, the deployer must inform you — and the accompanying recital says that should include the system's purpose, the type of decisions it makes, and your right to an explanation.

You can ask why. For an adverse decision based on such a system's output, you have a right to "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken."

You can complain. Any person with grounds to consider the Regulation has been infringed may complain to a national market surveillance authority — no standing test, no deadline. But the authority need only take the complaint into account, and no individual remedy attaches.

The date: 2 December 2027. The core high-risk regime for recruitment AI was postponed from August 2026 by the 2026 amending Regulation. The emotion-inference prohibition was not changed.

What you do not get: a private-sector employer owes you no fundamental-rights impact assessment for a hiring tool — that duty covers public bodies, private entities providing public services, and credit and insurance deployers only. And the worker-consultation duty applies to workers, not applicants.

Three things genuinely unresolved

1. Whether a job interview counts as "the workplace" for the emotion-inference prohibition. Neither text defines "workplace", and neither mentions applicants in that article. The stronger textual reading includes hiring — but it is not settled.

2. Whether the right to an explanation has practical effect before December 2027, since it applies from August 2026 while the rules classifying a system as high-risk do not.

3. Whether a job rejection "produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects" a candidate, which is a condition of that right.

The Commission owes guidelines that would probably settle the first. We have not read them, and this page does not guess at their content.

The same page also contains folk psychology — worth knowing why

Alongside genuinely useful local guidance (dress codes by sector, bring document copies, phone off, prepare a salary position), the page states:

That touching your mouth or nose is "a signal that you are most probably lying" — a claim directly contradicted by the deception research above.

That the first 20 seconds, 20 words and 20 gestures determine 80% of how an employer judges you. This is the only number in the article and it is unsourced.

That blue, brown, green and navy each "inspire trust," and that too much grey makes you appear indecisive with nothing interesting to offer. A signal sent by four of eight options is not a signal.

It also asks readers simultaneously to control their gestures and facial expressions and to behave naturally.

None of this is dishonest and none of it was presented as research. We include it because it is where a great many people actually get their interview advice — a government domain, first in search results, with a legal-currency banner and an explicit disclaimer that it is not a binding legal interpretation. Its only two external references are a commercial HR site and a CV-writing vendor.

The transferable skill: an official-looking source can be entirely right about local procedure and entirely wrong about human behaviour. The question is never who said it — it is what was measured.

Section 10The instrument was built in America — cross-cultural self-presentation

Modesty reads as missing data

The structured interview was built and validated in North America, and its anchored scales score personal action: a mid-range answer requires "clear personal action and an observable result." A candidate raised on a professional etiquette where the correct register is "we did it" — and where a Polish government portal lists "don't boast" among its interview commandments — answers with a collective account, and the rubric has nothing to attach a score to. Modesty doesn't read as modesty through this instrument; it reads as missing data. That consequence is our inference from the rubric mechanics, labelled as such.

The cross-cultural evidence is real and less flattering to folk wisdom than expected. A 10-country study (N = 3,509, Russia among the countries) found cultures differ in which self-presentation tactics candidates endorse — most of all on "individual excellence" — and that self-presentation was assigned more importance in hierarchical, high-embeddedness cultures with larger income disparities, with the American sample as the exception to the pattern. So "post-Soviet candidates undersell, Americans oversell" is folklore too; what differs is which tactics feel appropriate.

Claims we had in earlier drafts and removed

"Fabricated stories are inconsistent across interviews." Contradicted by the data — inventing qualifications was the second-most-stable behaviour measured.

Dark-trait correlations attributed to fabrication. Those figures belong to embellishment. Invention has its own, mostly smaller, column.

"Honest self-promotion is the effective tactic." The meta-analysis establishing that impression management raises ratings never separates honest from deceptive — its categories are self-focused versus other-focused. No source we have establishes that honesty specifically outperforms.

"Faking doesn't work." Contested and more interesting than that: it has a positive direct effect and a negative indirect one.

"Faking destroys interview validity." One study found academic performance was predicted better in a faking condition; its authors call the simple version "too simplified."

"The science, the vendors and the law all abandoned facial analysis." Three observations with three different scopes, collapsed into one false consensus. Separated on this page.

"Structure eliminates bias." The finding is no detected average difference in ratings, in one evidence set.

"AI and video make faking easier." Three studies point the other way.

Recruiters owe candidates a fundamental-rights impact assessment. They don't — that duty doesn't cover private-sector hiring.

GapsWhat this collection is missing

If you want to go further, these are the sources we most wished we had. All are peer-reviewed and, where noted, freely readable.

  • Bond & DePaulo (2006), "Accuracy of deception judgments" — the canonical meta-analysis showing humans detect deception at roughly 54%. The general version of the argument in Section 1.
  • Global Deception Research Team (2006), "A world of lies" — documents that "liars avert gaze" is the most widespread belief about lying across 75 countries. Freely available via PMC. The perfect companion to the eye-movement study: belief universal, cue invalid.
